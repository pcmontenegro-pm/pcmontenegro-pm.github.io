The full arc

A clinic's chaos became
a product thesis.

DermoSkin is a dermatology clinic running aesthetic and dermatological procedures — botulinum toxin, fillers, peelings, biostimulators — on a recurring, appointment-based model. Patient history lived in a paper folder. Appointments were tracked in a WhatsApp group. Money moved between a notebook and the owner's memory. Nothing talked to anything else, and nothing caught a contraindication before it became a risk.

I designed and built a complete internal operating system: patient records with structured health-history flags that surface automatically at the moment of booking, informed consent tracked as part of the appointment itself, stock control tied to what each service actually consumes, and a financial view that reconciles income, pending payments and instalments without a spreadsheet.

Running that system for a real clinic surfaced a pattern I'd seen before, in a different market, with Karigab → Órbita OS: the operational logic of a specific kind of business — here, a regulated, recurring, appointment-based practice — is specific, teachable, and repeatable. That's the thesis behind Filum, the multi-tenant SaaS I'm now building from what DermoSkin proved.

At a glance
  • The clinic: dermatology and aesthetic procedures, appointment-based, regulated by informed-consent requirements.
  • The system: patient records, agenda, informed consent, stock, financial control and WhatsApp/email follow-ups — one internal tool.

The problem

A contraindication is easy to miss
when it lives in a notebook.

DermoSkin's patient history — allergies, pregnancy, anticoagulant use, active infections, autoimmune conditions — lived on paper, one folder per patient, referenced from memory before a procedure. Appointments were booked through a WhatsApp thread scrolled by hand. Informed consent was a printed form, signed on paper, filed loosely. Every payment — full, partial, or in instalments — was tracked in a personal notebook only the owner could interpret.

None of this failed loudly. It failed quietly: a follow-up that never got booked, a contraindication that surfaced after the appointment was already confirmed, a partial payment nobody remembered to chase. The risk wasn't visible until it was too late to act on it.

"The system didn't need more features. It needed to put the one fact that mattered — this patient has a contraindication — in front of the right person at the right moment."
Observation from discovery
What was built

Six modules.
One operating system.

01
Patient records & contraindication alerts
A structured intake for pregnancy, allergies, anticoagulants, active infection, autoimmune conditions and underlying disease — each with room for detail. Any flag raises a visible alert that follows the patient into every future booking, until someone explicitly reviews it.
Patient safety
02
Appointment-linked informed consent
Six consent groups matched to procedure type, generated per appointment, shareable by WhatsApp or QR, tracked as pending or signed — replacing a loose stack of printed forms with a record tied to the actual visit.
Compliance
03
Stock tied to services, not guesswork
Every service declares what it consumes per session. Completing an appointment prompts a real consumption check against the suggested amount, and stock alerts trigger automatically once supply drops below a safety threshold.
Inventory
04
Financial control without a spreadsheet
Every appointment generates its own income record — paid, pending, or split into instalments — reconciled against a monthly net view, with an audit trail for manual corrections.
Finance
05
Follow-up & retouch reminders
Services with a retouch window or repetition cycle generate their own reminder automatically, surfaced on the clinic's home screen with a ready-to-send WhatsApp or email message — so a follow-up depends on the system, not on memory.
Retention
06
Role-based access for a growing team
An administrator role and an assistant role with configurable permissions — editing the service catalogue, deactivating services, deleting patients or financial records — so the clinic can bring on staff without losing control of sensitive actions.

What's next

From DermoSkin's system
to Filum.

DermoSkin's operating system was built for one clinic, but the logic underneath it — contraindication-aware patient records, appointment-linked consent, service-tied stock, reconciled financials — isn't specific to DermoSkin. It's specific to a category: regulated, recurring, appointment-based practices.

Filum is that logic, generalised into a multi-tenant SaaS platform, currently in development. DermoSkin is its first real-world reference — the same pattern behind Karigab → Órbita OS, applied to healthtech.

Why this matters

The broader relevance.

This work sits at the intersection of three dynamics shaping regulated service businesses right now.

Regulated practices are underserved by generic tools
Generic scheduling and CRM tools don't model contraindication alerts or appointment-linked consent. A system built around the actual clinical and regulatory shape of the practice does more with less — and catches the risk generic tools were never designed to see.
The operating system comes before the SaaS
Same pattern as Karigab → Órbita OS: prove the operational logic works for one real business before generalising it into a product. Filum inherits its shape from what DermoSkin actually runs on — not from a feature wishlist.
Visibility at the right moment is a safety feature
Surfacing the one fact that matters — right when it matters — is a pattern that generalises beyond clinics, to any regulated, appointment-based service business with liability-sensitive intake.
