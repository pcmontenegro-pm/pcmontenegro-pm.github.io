What this screen solves
Safety-first routing
Google Maps routes by time. Strava routes by performance. CycleSafe routes by safety — the gap no existing tool filled.
Three-layer taxonomy
Red = ciclovía (dedicated lane), green = community-validated safe, amber = mixed traffic. Simple enough to read while riding.
No account required to view
First-session value without friction. Users see the map immediately — registration gated only for reporting.
PM decision
The route card shows a 74% safe score — a single number that aggregates community reports for that route segment. This was the most debated V1 feature: does a score create false confidence? Discovery interviews suggested users wanted a number, not just colours. We shipped it and monitored.
Why a community feed?
Social proof of data quality
Seeing 12 upvotes on a hazard report tells you other cyclists verified it. Raw community data without social validation creates noise.
Return trigger without push
The feed gives users a reason to open the app when not navigating — browsing their neighbourhood's recent reports. Engagement without notifications.
Community identity
Named contributors (not anonymous) increased contribution rate. Cyclists wanted credit for helping others — gamification was unnecessary.
PM decision
Filters (All / Hazards / Safe / Updates / Near me) were added after pilot feedback. Without filters, the feed felt overwhelming after 20+ reports per day. "Near me" became the default for 80% of active users.
Contribution design principles
Maximum 3 taps to submit
Type → safety level → submit. The note field is optional and explicitly labelled as such. Every required field added drops contribution rate.
Post-ride prompt, not mid-ride
Reports are triggered after ride completion — never during navigation. Safety of the user comes before data collection.
4 types, not 20
Early prototype had 12 hazard categories. Pilot showed users ignored most — they wanted to flag fast, not classify precisely. Collapsed to 4.
Core learning from pilot
Contribution rate dropped 60% after week 2. The initial novelty of reporting wore off. The unsolved problem: creating durable contribution habits without gamification or incentives. This is the network effect challenge that paused the project.
Why show contribution stats?
Contributor identity, not leaderboard
The profile shows your contribution history — not a ranking. Discovery interviews found leaderboards felt competitive; personal history felt purposeful.
Safe score history
Each saved ride shows a safety score — building a personal record of route choices over time. This creates utility for returning users beyond the map alone.
Upvotes as impact signal
284 upvotes means 284 cyclists found your reports useful. This was the most motivating stat for active contributors — seeing their impact on the community.
Not built in V1
Badges, streaks, and ride challenges were fully designed but deliberately excluded from MVP. The goal was to validate intrinsic community motivation before layering gamification. Pilot confirmed cyclists would contribute without rewards — making gamification a V2 decision, not a V1 dependency.
