Executive Summary

What CycleSafe is.

CycleSafe is a community-powered mobile platform designed to help urban cyclists make safer route decisions. Conceived and built in Argentina in 2024, the product addressed a gap that established navigation tools like Google Maps and Strava were not solving: efficient routes are not the same as safe routes.

I led the product as Product Lead — from initial discovery through pilot launch and validation. The project ran as a remote collaboration and reached a functioning pilot with 40 registered users before I relocated to Ireland in early 2025.

The Problem · Market Gap

Navigation tools optimise for speed.
Cyclists ride for survival.

Cycling infrastructure in Argentine cities is uneven, rapidly changing, and highly local. A route that was safe six months ago may have a new construction site, removed cycle lane markings, or a newly dangerous intersection. No existing tool aggregated this knowledge at the street level — from the people who actually ride those streets every day.

Existing tools addressed safety perception in the Argentine market. Google Maps, Strava, Komoot — all optimise for distance or performance. None surfaced community-reported hazards.

Cycling adoption accelerated post-pandemic in Buenos Aires, creating a rapidly growing segment of new urban cyclists without local safety knowledge — exactly the users who need this most.
Discovery · User Research

What we heard
in the interviews.

Discovery interviews focused on understanding how urban cyclists currently make route decisions — which tools they use, what information they trust, and where they feel the biggest gaps are. The central hypothesis: perceived safety is as important as time or distance when choosing a cycling route.

Finding 01
Trust lived over official
Cyclists consistently trusted advice from other riders over any app recommendation. A WhatsApp message from someone in their cycling group carried more weight than a Google Maps route — because local knowledge was current, specific, and came from someone with skin in the game.
Finding 02
Safety is local and time-sensitive
What makes a street safe at 7am is different from what makes it safe at 7pm. Infrastructure conditions, traffic patterns, lighting, and even weather affect perception. Static maps fail here — the information stales within days. Community-reported data stays current because it reflects real conditions in real time.
Finding 03
Willingness to contribute was high — if easy
Cyclists expressed strong willingness to share safety information — but only if the contribution required minimal friction. A 10-tap rating process would fail. A one-swipe 'this street felt safe / unsafe' submission would work. Contribution design was as important as display design.
Core Features · Product Value

Community intelligence.
Layered on a map.

01
Safety Route Map
The core interface displayed cycle paths, cyclist-friendly streets, and mixed-traffic zones with a colour-coded safety taxonomy — green for validated safe, amber for mixed, red for community-reported risk. Users could visualise their city's cycling reality at a glance.
PM rationale: First-session value. No account required to view.
02
Community Reporting
Riders could flag route segments after completing a journey — reporting hazards, praising safe infrastructure, or marking active risk zones. Contribution was designed for minimum friction: a single post-ride prompt with a swipe interface to classify the experience.
PM rationale: Solves cold start — every ride generates data.
03
Saved Routes
Users could save frequently used routes and receive notifications when community members reported new safety conditions on those specific paths. This transformed passive map users into an engaged, returning audience with a direct personal stake in the data quality.
PM rationale: Retention driver. Creates habit without pushing.
04
Ride Tracking
A basic ride recording feature allowed users to log journeys, visualise their own cycling history, and — crucially — generate data that fed back into the community map. Every tracked ride was an implicit safety report for the segments covered.
PM rationale: Strava-familiar behaviour. No new habit required.
05
Community Feed
A lightweight activity stream showed recent reports from the community — new hazards flagged, routes validated, and updates from nearby riders. This created social proof and gave users a reason to open the app beyond active navigation.
PM rationale: Drives organic return without notifications.
06
Deferred: Multi-city Expansion
Scaling to multiple cities was explicitly deferred from V1. The priority was validating the core loop — user generates data, data improves map, map attracts users — in a single dense urban context before expanding. Buenos Aires provided the right density to test this.
PM rationale: Network effects require density before geography.

Onboarding Flow
Welcome & taxonomy
The first thing a user sees. Three colour codes, one sentence each. The entire safety system explained before they touch the map.
Onboarding flow · Screen 1 of 9
Design decisions
Taxonomy before map
Users who see coloured lines without context misinterpret them. Teaching the three-tier system on entry eliminates confusion at first use — the biggest source of early drop-off in prototype testing.
One screen, not a carousel
Multi-screen onboarding flows were consistently skipped. All three concepts on one page, scannable in under 10 seconds. Tested against a 3-screen carousel — single screen won on comprehension.
Location framed as benefit
Allow location CTA positioned after taxonomy explanation — after the user understands why location matters. Permission conversion rate increased significantly when framed as product benefit, not system requirement.
PM decision
No account creation on first launch. View the map immediately, report after sign-up. Forcing registration before value delivery was the single biggest conversion killer in comparable community apps.
Navigation flow · Screen 2 of 9
What this screen solves
Safety-first routing
Google Maps routes by time. Strava by performance. CycleSafe by safety — the gap no existing tool filled for urban cyclists in Buenos Aires.
Two interactive entry points
Tap the search bar to find destinations. Tap the route card to see street-level community data. Both discoverable without instruction.
Live alert on active route
The ⚠ Alert zone pill reflects a real community report from the last 2 hours. Freshness is surfaced explicitly — not buried in a report list.
PM decision
The 74% safe score was the most debated V1 feature. Does a single number create false confidence? Discovery interviews said cyclists wanted a number to compare routes quickly. We shipped it and monitored. No misuse patterns emerged in the pilot.
Navigation flow · Screen 3 of 9
Route search design
Safe score as primary sort
Standard nav apps sort by distance or time. CycleSafe sorts by safe score — the core value proposition embedded in the most-used flow. 88% vs 52% safe for the same destination makes the choice obvious.
Three options, not one
Fastest, safest, and alternative always presented together. Pilot users consistently chose the safer route even when it added 5+ minutes — directly validating the core hypothesis.
Recent searches for commuters
Cyclists reuse the same 2–3 routes. Recent searches surface instantly — reducing time-to-map for daily users to under 2 taps.
PM decision
Routes named as origin → destination, not street names. "Palermo → Microcentro" is how cyclists think. "Callao → Av. de Mayo via Santa Fe" is how engineers think. Naming from the user's mental model, not the system's.
Navigation flow · Screen 4 of 9
Route-level data layer
Segment-level safe score
Each street segment has its own score updated continuously. The route card shows the weighted average. The detail screen shows the breakdown — including how many reports feed the score.
Named contributors build trust
Reports show names, not anonymous entries. Pilot showed named reports were upvoted 3x more. Cyclists trust other cyclists — not an abstract data system.
Relative timestamps communicate freshness
"34 min ago" vs "Yesterday" changes how a cyclist acts on a report. A fresh hazard is credible. A 3-day-old one may be resolved. Relative time, not absolute dates.
PM decision
Reports older than 48 hours are grayed out, not deleted. Historical data builds the safety profile of a segment over time. Stale data preserves signal. Freshness made obvious at a glance without losing the record.
Navigation flow · Screen 5 of 9
Active ride design principles
Safety before stats
The alert zone warning appears above navigation instruction. While riding in city traffic, a hazard ahead is more important than current speed. Visual hierarchy reflects real-world priority.
Strava-familiar stat format
Distance, time, speed — the three stats every cyclist already tracks. No new mental model required. Familiar patterns drive adoption without training.
Passive tracking — minimal interaction
No buttons beyond "End ride" are primary during navigation. A cyclist in traffic should not be tapping a screen. The app tracks passively; contribution happens after the ride, never during.
PM decision
The red RECORDING badge was deliberate UX, not decoration. 30% of early prototype users thought the app had stopped tracking mid-ride. The persistent badge reduced "is it recording?" confusion to zero in the pilot.
Contribution flow · Screen 6 of 9
The highest-leverage screen in the product
Where the data layer is built
Without this moment working, the map stays empty. Every design decision here — the safe score, the social framing, the "20 seconds" promise — exists to convert ride completion into community contribution.
Social framing, not obligation
"Your report helps 40 cyclists tomorrow" — not "help keep the map accurate." Contribution framed as direct community impact. Pilot showed 2x higher submission rate with social framing vs. platform maintenance framing.
Skip always visible
Forced contribution flows damage trust. "Skip for now" is prominent. Voluntary contribution is sustainable. Coerced reports are low quality and users who feel forced churn faster.
Core pilot learning
Contribution rate: 68% in week 1, dropped to 31% by week 4. Novelty wore off. The unsolved problem: a durable habit loop without gamification. Streak mechanics were designed for V2 but never shipped before the project paused.
Contribution flow · Screen 7 of 9
Contribution design principles
Maximum 3 taps to submit
Type → safety level → submit. Every additional required field reduced contribution rate measurably in prototype testing. Note is optional and labelled as such.
4 types, not 12
Early prototype had 12 hazard categories. Pilots showed users wanted to flag fast, not classify precisely. Collapsed to 4 based on the most common report types from pilot week 1-2.
Type and severity are independent
A construction zone can be Safe (minor, temporary) or Risk (full blockage). Separating type from severity gives the data model more signal without increasing cognitive load.
PM decision
Location is auto-detected from GPS, never typed. Asking users to type a street name killed 40% of reports in prototype testing. The context of where you are while riding is the most reliable location signal — and it's already there.
Community flow · Screen 8 of 9
Why a community feed?
Return trigger without push notifications
The feed gives users a reason to open the app between rides. Browsing neighbourhood safety reports is a habit independent of navigation — and works without notification permission.
Upvotes as quality signal
12 upvotes on a hazard report tells other cyclists it was verified. Social validation replaces moderation — the community curates quality without platform intervention.
"Near me" became the default
Post-pilot: 80% of active users filtered to "Near me" within their first 3 sessions. City-wide feed felt overwhelming. Neighbourhood scale felt immediately relevant and actionable.
PM decision
Named contributors, not anonymous. Pilot showed named reports were upvoted 3x more and acted on more frequently. Identity signals accountability — cyclists trust other cyclists, not a data system.
Profile flow · Screen 9 of 9
Profile design philosophy
Personal history, not leaderboard
Discovery interviews: leaderboards felt competitive and stressful. Personal history felt purposeful. The profile shows your impact on your community, not your rank against others.
Safe score per ride
Each saved ride shows the safety score for that specific journey. 91% safe on Tuesday means you chose well. Creates utility for returning users beyond navigation alone.
284 upvotes = community impact
The most motivating stat for active contributors in pilot interviews. Not rides logged, not km covered — how many cyclists found your reports useful. Impact, not activity metrics.
Not built in V1
Badges, streaks, and challenges were fully designed but deliberately excluded from MVP. Goal: validate intrinsic motivation before gamification. Pilot confirmed cyclists contribute without rewards — making gamification a V2 decision, not a V1 dependency.

Execution · Project Phases

Discovery to pilot
in three phases.

Phase 1 · Q2 2024
Discovery & problem validation
Exploratory interviews with urban cyclists in Buenos Aires. Competitive analysis of Google Maps, Strava, Komoot, and local cycle apps. Hypothesis definition: safety perception as primary routing criterion. Vision and strategy alignment with co-founders.
Completed
Phase 2 · Q3–Q4 2024
MVP build & branding
MVP scoped and prioritised: community map, route reporting, ride tracking. React Native development. Safety taxonomy defined (ciclovía / mixed / risk). Visual identity built: red brand inspired by Strava's high-energy positioning. Internal testing and QA before pilot.
Completed
Phase 3 · Q4 2024–Q1 2025
Pilot launch & learning
Pilot launched with 40 registered users in Buenos Aires. Adoption metrics, contribution rates, and qualitative feedback collected. Key hypotheses validated: safety is a meaningful routing criterion and cyclists will contribute data. Project paused when I relocated to Ireland.
Pilot completed · Scaling paused
Pilot Results · What We Learned

40 users.
Enough to learn what matters.

The pilot's objective was never scale — it was learning. 40 users in a dense urban context was enough to observe contribution behaviour, test the core loop, and identify where the network effect would need to be solved before any expansion.

40 Registered pilot users
Buenos Aires urban cyclists recruited via cycling community channels.
✓ Core hypothesis validated
Users understood the value proposition immediately. Safety-first routing resonated strongly across all interview segments.
↑ Willingness to contribute data
Active users contributed route reports without prompting — the community motivation was real, not induced by incentives.
What worked
Safety framing resonated immediately. Users grasped the core value in seconds — 'this shows where it's safe to ride' needed no explanation.
Genuine willingness to contribute. Cyclists wanted to help other cyclists. Community motivation proved real without requiring gamification or rewards.
Familiar interaction model. Strava-like ride tracking reduced the learning curve. Users arrived with relevant mental models already formed.
What we discovered
Content requires critical mass. A map with sparse data is less useful than a map with no data — it creates false confidence. Density of reports matters more than breadth.
Data freshness is everything. Safety conditions change faster than expected. A month-old report about a construction zone can actively mislead a rider.
Contribution friction has a ceiling. Users contributed enthusiastically in the first two weeks. Sustaining that behaviour required stronger hooks than the MVP contained.
What wasn't resolved
Network effect threshold. The minimum number of active contributors to make a zone's map genuinely useful was never reached. This is the core unsolved problem.
Multi-city strategy. Expanding to Córdoba or Rosario would have diluted the already thin Buenos Aires data. The right sequencing for geographic expansion was undefined.
Sustainability of data quality. Community-generated data degraded without active curation. The moderation and verification model was designed but not tested at scale.
PM Learnings · Transferable Principles

Three lessons I carry
from CycleSafe.

Validate the hypothesis before solving the chicken-and-egg
Community platforms always face the cold-start problem. But the cold-start problem is only worth solving if the core value hypothesis holds. CycleSafe validated that cyclists want and will use safety-first routing — which made the network effect challenge worth attacking. Knowing what you're actually building for matters more than building it fast.
Network effects require density, not geography
The instinct is to expand coverage as fast as possible — more cities, more reach. The correct instinct is to go deep before going wide. A hyper-dense, highly accurate map of one neighbourhood is more valuable to its users than a thin map of an entire city. Geographic expansion is a reward for solving density, not a precondition for it.
Real-time data products decay without curation infrastructure
A safety map is only as useful as its most recent accurate report. Unlike static products, the CycleSafe map could actively harm users if stale — an outdated 'safe' tag on a now-dangerous street is worse than no tag at all. Data freshness and verification needed to be designed as features, not assumed as outcomes of community contribution.
Contribution design is as important as consumption design
Most product design effort goes into how users consume information — the map, the route, the feed. For community platforms, the design of how users contribute information is equally critical. Every tap added to the reporting flow had a measurable cost in contribution rate. The MVP taught me to design contribution flows with the same rigour as core user journeys.
Recruiter Relevance

Why CycleSafe matters
for your team.

The product thinking demonstrated here — network effects, community mechanics, data quality at scale — is directly applicable to marketplace, platform, and data-driven product roles.

Marketplace & platform products
Managing the supply-demand balance of a community map — contributors versus consumers — is the same discipline as managing marketplace liquidity. The cold-start problem, network effects, and contribution incentive design are first-principles marketplace PM skills.
Data product management
CycleSafe's core challenge was data quality at scale — freshness, accuracy, and trust in community-generated information. These are the same questions that face any product where the data layer is the product: financial data aggregators, fraud detection systems, open banking platforms.
0-to-1 product leadership
CycleSafe went from hypothesis to functioning pilot without an established team, budget, or market validation. Navigating ambiguity, making irreversible decisions with incomplete data, and shipping a real product used by real people in a live market — this is the experience that matters in early-stage or greenfield product roles.
