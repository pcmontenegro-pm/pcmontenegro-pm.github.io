Executive Summary

One integration.
Every bank.

Corporate clients of Itaú Chile needed to receive high-value payments from their own customers — who held accounts at different Chilean banks. Without a unified solution, each bank required a separate integration or manual workaround, creating fragmentation, friction and reconciliation complexity.

The Multi-Bank Payment Button created a single integration model — API for technically capable clients, web portal for operational users — enabling payment initiation across multiple Chilean banks through one access point, with same-day payment capabilities.

The Problem

High-value payments held back
by integration complexity.

Corporate clients handling large transactions — with average tickets above CLP 50 million — were limited by the fragmentation of the Chilean banking system. Their payers used different banks, and there was no single way to receive those payments without building and maintaining separate bank relationships or manual processes.

The result: payment delays, reconciliation overhead, and a product offering that couldn't compete with the simplicity of a single integrated solution. The commercial opportunity was clear — but the product had to be built from scratch.

Product Challenge

What made it difficult.

01 Multi-bank technical integration: Each bank had its own API specifications, authentication models and message formats. A single abstraction layer had to handle all of them reliably.
02 Dual access model design: Different corporate clients had different technical capabilities. Designing a product that worked equally well as an API integration and as a web portal — without compromising either — required deliberate trade-off decisions.
03 High-value transaction risk: Payments at CLP 50M+ ticket size required careful error handling, payment confirmation design and reconciliation logic — where a missed or duplicated transaction had significant commercial and operational consequences.
04 Corporate client onboarding: The target segment included sophisticated corporate clients with their own IT and finance teams. The onboarding experience had to match the expectations of enterprise buyers, not retail users.
My Role

Defining the product
from first principles.

I led product definition for the Multi-Bank Payment Button from the initial commercial opportunity through to pilot rollout. This included defining the integration architecture requirements, the dual access model, the onboarding journey for corporate clients, and the pilot selection criteria.

I worked closely with technology teams on the API design and with commercial teams on the go-to-market approach, while coordinating operational and risk requirements across the product lifecycle.

Key Decisions

What I decided.

01 Dual access model from day one: Rather than building API-only and adding a portal later, both access channels were defined in the initial scope — ensuring we served both technically capable and operationally-focused corporate clients from the first release.
02 Pilot with three selected companies: Rather than a broad launch, I recommended starting with three corporate clients that represented different integration profiles — API integrators, portal users, and hybrid — to surface the broadest set of real issues before scaling.
03 Payment confirmation as a first-class feature: At high-value ticket sizes, ambiguity about whether a payment was received — or was pending, failed, or duplicated — was unacceptable. I prioritised payment status clarity over speed of the initial release.
04 Target 40 corporate clients in the first year: Set a specific, measurable adoption target with the commercial team — not just 'launch and see'. This created shared accountability between product and commercial for the rollout phase.
Product Design · Dual Access Model

One product.
Two ways to access it.

The key product insight was that corporate clients have fundamentally different integration capabilities. A single access model would have excluded one group or created a poor experience for the other. The dual access model resolved this without creating two separate products.

Access model 1
API Integration
For corporate clients with IT capability and existing payment infrastructure. Direct system-to-system integration with Itaú's multi-bank API. Enables automated payment initiation, real-time status retrieval and programmatic reconciliation.
For: Technology-led corporate clients
Access model 2
Web Portal
For corporate clients whose finance or operations teams manage payments directly. A web-based interface for payment initiation, status monitoring and basic reconciliation — no IT integration required. Enables faster commercial onboarding.
For: Operations-led corporate clients

European Relevance

Why this translates to European roles.

Open Banking & PSD2 payment initiation
The Multi-Bank Payment Button is structurally equivalent to PSD2 payment initiation — a single integration layer enabling payment from multiple bank accounts. The product challenges are the same: multi-bank normalisation, dual access models and enterprise onboarding.
B2B payment orchestration
GoCardless, Modulr, Volt, and similar players in Europe are solving the same problem — abstracting bank complexity behind a single API for corporate clients. Building this product in a regulated banking context demonstrates the PM depth that these roles require.
Enterprise product management
Managing a product pilot with corporate clients — who have their own IT, finance and procurement processes — requires different PM skills than consumer products. Defining onboarding journeys, API documentation requirements and pilot selection criteria for enterprise buyers is a specific capability.
Product Learnings

What this shows about how I work.

Different clients need different access models. A single access model would have forced corporate clients into an experience that didn't fit their actual working environment. Designing for two different user types from the beginning was the correct call — not scope creep.

Pilots are discovery, not validation. The three-company pilot was treated as a structured learning exercise — not just a technical test. Each client was selected to represent a different integration profile, giving us coverage across the range of real-world conditions we'd face at scale.

At high-value tickets, confirmation is a product feature. When a transaction is CLP 50M+, the moment between payment initiation and confirmed receipt carries real risk. Designing clear payment status states — pending, confirmed, failed, duplicate — was a core product decision, not a UI detail.

Adoption targets create shared accountability. Setting a specific target of 40 corporate clients in year one wasn't just a metric — it created a shared commercial and product conversation that didn't exist before. Without a number, 'rollout' becomes indefinitely deferrable.
