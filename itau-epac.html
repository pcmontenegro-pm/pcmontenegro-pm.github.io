ePAC — Digital Direct Debit

Digitising a legacy paper-based direct debit mandate process to reduce activation friction and accelerate recurring payment onboarding.

Senior Product Owner Recurring Payments 3-month delivery
🔒 Metrics are directional. Sensitive commercial detail omitted.
Executive Summary

From paper to digital.

The ePAC initiative replaced a paper-based direct debit mandate process that was creating significant friction in customer onboarding. Activation times stretched across weeks, requiring manual handling at multiple steps, and leading to drop-off before customers could start using the recurring payment service.

The product outcome was a fully digital mandate flow — enabling online or near-24-hour activation — in partnership with an external provider. The initiative required aligning legal, operations, commercial and technology teams around a new activation model.

The Problem

Weeks of friction
before first payment.

Direct debit is a recurring payment model where mandate activation is the critical first step. If that step is slow, manual or opaque, customers abandon before the service starts generating value — for them or for the bank.

The legacy paper mandate process created a gap of weeks between a customer expressing intent and their first successful direct debit — a gap filled with manual effort, operational risk and commercial attrition.

Product Challenge

What made it difficult.

01 Regulatory constraints: Mandate validity requirements imposed legal and compliance obligations on the digital signature format and storage.
02 External partner dependency: Digital mandate activation required integrating with Forpay — an external provider — which added coordination, technical and contractual complexity.
03 Multi-team alignment: Legal, operations, commercial and technology teams each had a stake in the outcome. Coordinating priorities and sign-offs across four distinct departments was as much of the work as the product itself.
04 Customer eligibility: Not all customer segments were immediately eligible for the digital flow. The rollout had to be phased, with eligibility criteria defined and communicated carefully.
My Role

Senior Product Owner — end to end.

I owned the product from discovery through to rollout monitoring. This included defining the digital mandate flow, managing the Forpay partnership from the product side, coordinating stakeholder alignment across four teams, and establishing the acceptance criteria and eligibility model for the phased launch.

I was the point of coordination between what legal required, what operations could handle, what the commercial team expected, and what technology could deliver in the 3-month window.

Key Decisions

What I decided.

01 Partner-first model: Rather than building digital signature capability in-house, I recommended integrating with an established external provider — reducing delivery time and regulatory risk.
02 Phased eligibility rollout: Defined the initial eligible customer segment carefully to manage operational and legal risk during the first release, with a clear expansion plan.
03 Parallel paper process retained: Kept the paper mandate as a fallback for non-eligible customers during the transition, avoiding a hard cutover that would have increased operational risk.
04 Activation monitoring built from day one: Defined tracking for activation completion rates from the first pilot cohort, enabling rapid iteration based on real usage rather than assumptions.

Product Learnings

What this shows about how I work.

Friction is a product decision. The paper mandate wasn't broken — it was just designed for a different era. Treating it as a product problem rather than an operational problem opened up the space to design a better solution.

Partner integrations require product ownership, not just coordination. Managing the Forpay integration meant defining the product requirements, acceptance criteria and testing scope — not just tracking a third-party delivery.

Phased rollout is risk management. Launching with a defined eligible segment — rather than a full cutover — was the decision that made the initiative survivable. It gave us real data, controlled the blast radius, and built organisational confidence in the new model.

Alignment is a product deliverable. In a regulated bank with multiple stakeholders, getting four teams to agree on a single operating model — before writing a single line of code — was as valuable as the product itself.
